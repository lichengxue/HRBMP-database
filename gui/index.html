Development Disclaimer

This portal is a development interface for reviewing HRBMP database structure, metadata, data-access workflows, and map display concepts. Example records, availability layers, and downloads should not be interpreted as an official HRBMP data release until reviewed datasets and release rules are finalized.

Program Description

Purpose

This GUI organizes HRBMP information around monitoring stations, sampling events, biological observations, environmental observations, and source metadata. The portal keeps the workflow transparent for scientific users while the database structure is still being refined.

Data Workflow

  1. Load validated source tables into SQLite.
  2. Export analysis-ready JSON summaries for the static GUI.
  3. Review coverage by station, time period, taxonomic group, and data type.
  4. Update records with reviewed HRBMP data and documentation.

Educational Materials

K-12 Curriculum

A Hudson River learning pathway organized by student age, classroom readiness, and scientific skill level.

Curriculum Pathway

The sequence moves from observation and vocabulary in early grades to authentic data analysis and evidence-based environmental decision-making in high school.

K-2 Grades 3-5 Grades 6-8 Grades 9-12 Teacher Resources
K-2: River Explorers

Observation-rich lessons for young students learning what living things need and how animals use body parts to survive.

Meet Hudson River Animals

Students sort fish, crabs, and other river organisms by observable features, then draw what each animal needs to live.

Fish Body Parts

Students label fins, eyes, mouth, tail, and body shape, then explain how those parts help a fish move, feed, and hide.

Grades 3-5: Habitat Detectives

Elementary investigations that connect life cycles, habitat, river regions, and simple graphs from monitoring records.

Fish Life Stages

Students compare eggs, larvae, juveniles, and adults, then match life stages to habitat needs in the estuary.

Where Are The Fish?

Students use HRBMP river regions to map species observations and build simple bar charts by region or season.

Grades 6-8: Estuary Data Lab

Middle school modules where students ask questions, filter database records, interpret patterns, and connect fish distributions to environmental conditions.

Biodiversity By Region

Students compare species richness and abundance across Hudson River regions and discuss how sampling design affects conclusions.

Water Quality And Fish

Students compare temperature, dissolved oxygen, salinity, and fish records to identify possible habitat relationships.

Grades 9-12: Hudson River Research Lab

High school investigations focused on authentic monitoring questions, data limitations, evidence, and communication of scientific claims.

Long-Term Change

Students analyze annual or seasonal patterns in selected species and evaluate whether the evidence supports a trend claim.

From Data To Decision

Students build a short research brief using database results, uncertainty, and data-sharing expectations for responsible science.

Teacher Resources

Planning materials that can be added as downloadable packets as the curriculum matures.

Lesson Packet Templates

Space for printable lessons, student worksheets, answer keys, vocabulary lists, and slide decks.

Standards Alignment

Space for NY P-12 and NGSS-aligned summaries covering practices, ecosystems, Earth systems, and human activity.

Alewife

Alosa pseudoharengus

Did you know?

It is thought that a fish will return to reproduce in the same river in which they were born

Appearance

  • Large eye and relatively deep body
  • Black spot located behind shoulder
  • Forked tail

Life history

  • Spend most of life in saltwater and reproduce in freshwater
  • Reproduction in the Hudson River occurs from late March to early May

Diet

  • Fish eggs and larval fish
  • Insects
  • Zooplankton

Role in the Hudson

  • Component of the forage base for coastal fishes, marine mammals and seabirds, but also terrestrial animals and shorebirds

Why we monitor

  • Ecologically and economically important species
  • Population changes can reveal broader ecosystem level changes

American Shad

Alosa sapidissima

Did you know?

The Shadbush tree is named after this fish because the spring bloom aligns with the return of shad for river reproduction

Appearance

  • Thin metallic body varying in color from greenish to dark blue
  • Large dark shoulder spot may be followed by several paler spots
  • Deeply forked tail

Life history

  • Reproduction in the Hudson River occurs on sandy, gravelly shoals and other shallow areas
  • A female may release 100,000 to 600,000 eggs

Diet

  • Juveniles eat zooplankton and terrestrial insects
  • Feed on plankton, crustaceans, and small fish in the ocean

Role in the Hudson

  • Migratory link between the river and open ocean – transporting nutrients between the systems
  • Food source to larger fish, birds, and some terrestrial animals

Why we monitor

  • Historically very abundant and now population reduced to low levels – important to track population recovery

Atlantic Sturgeon

Acipenser oxyrinchus

Did you know?

This is the largest sturgeon species found in New York

Appearance

  • Have a series of bony plates called scutes on their back, sides, and belly
  • Can reach 14 feet in length

Life history

  • Adults return to the Hudson River to spawn in May and June
  • Young fish remain in their natal river for up to 6 years before swimming out to the ocean

Diet

  • Bottom-feeders that primarily eat crustaceans, mollusks, insects, and worms

Role in the Hudson

  • Regulate benthic prey populations

Why we monitor

  • Listed under the Endangered Species Act and population recovery is a key goal

Atlantic Tomcod

Microgadus tomcod

Did you know?

Have developed the ability to produce antifreeze proteins making them very tolerant of cold water temperatures

Appearance

  • Reach up to 9-12 inches in length
  • Three distinct dorsal fins and two anal fins
  • Single barbel on chin

Life history

  • Rarely live past 1 year old in the Hudson River
  • Primarily demersal and reproduce from November to February over gravel and sand

Diet

  • Juveniles eat copepods and small amphipods
  • Adults eat small crustaceans, shellfish, worms, and other fish

Role in the Hudson

  • Important prey for larger predatory fish

Why we monitor

  • Can be used for tracing chemical contaminants as they tend to accumulate high concentrations in polluted areas

Bay Anchovy

Anchoa mitchilli

Did you know?

Adults may live to be slightly older than 3 years old, but few often survive past their first year

Appearance

  • Slender and grows up to 4 inches in length
  • Silvery-green with a translucent body and a silver stripe on its sides

Life history

  • Schooling fish
  • Can reach maturity at around 2.5 months
  • Reproduce in the nighttime between April and September

Diet

  • Fish less than 25 mm eat planktonic copepods and larvae
  • Larger fish eat amphipods, ostracods, and small mollusks

Role in the Hudson

  • Important forage fish for many other birds and recreationally and commercially important fishes

Why we monitor

  • Good indicator of estuary pollution stress

Blueback Herring

Alosa aestivalis

Did you know?

Blueback herring and alewife are collectively termed “river herring”

Appearance

  • Bluish green top coloration and overall silvery color
  • Usually have a dark spot located just behind the gill plate on the upper side of the body

Life history

  • Schooling species
  • Reproduce in the Hudson River during late spring and early summer

Diet

  • Planktivore – consumes copepods, pelagic shrimp larvae and larval fishes

Role in the Hudson

  • Important food web link – eat plankton and they themselves are then eaten by larger predatory fish and birds

Why we monitor

  • Important forage base for larger predators

Bluefish

Pomatomus saltatrix

Did you know?

Baby bluefish are known as snappers

Appearance

  • Steel to greenish blue color on upper parts, fading to silver on lower sides and belly
  • Forked tail and streamlined body with sharp teeth

Life history

  • Live up to 12 years
  • Reproduce multiple times in the spring and summer
  • Females can produce over 400,000 eggs or more

Diet

  • Fish, including bay anchovy and silversides
  • Invertebrates

Role in the Hudson

  • Voracious apex predator fish regulating smaller forage fish populations

Why we monitor

  • Assess population status
  • Track PCB contamination levels

Channel Catfish

Ictalurus punctatus

Did you know?

Have taste receptors all over their body, with the highest concentration located on the four pairs of barbels (“whiskers”)

Appearance

  • Bluish or greenish gray body with a silvery white belly and olive fins
  • Four pairs of barbels around mouth

Life history

  • Parents build nests like under rocks or in submerged hollow logs and guard young
  • Life span varies greatly but some may live for over 25 years

Diet

  • Bottom feeders eating aquatic insects, arthropods, and fish

Role in the Hudson

  • Invasive species that could outcompete native catfish species like the white catfish

Why we monitor

  • Non-native invasive species impacts

Gizzard Shad

Dorosoma cepedianum

Did you know?

Named for its unique muscular stomach that functions like a bird’s gizzard – use ingested sand or small stones to grind up food

Appearance

  • Small head and mouth with a short and blunt snout
  • Greatly elongated last dorsal ray
  • Single faint spot on shoulder

Life history

  • Reproduce near the surface during mid to late spring
  • Occur mainly in freshwater but can be found in brackish or saline waters

Diet

  • Newly hatched larvae eat protozoans and other plankton
  • Diet later includes detritus, algae, and phytoplankton

Role in the Hudson

  • Forage fish for valued game fishes

Why we monitor

  • Tracking population changes can reveal information about broader ecosystem changes

Hogchoker

Trinectes maculatus

Did you know?

It is said that when farmers would feed the fish to pigs, they would choke on them due to the rough scales, hence their name

Appearance

  • Right-eyed flatfish
  • Brownish-gray body with vertical lines and spots

Life history

  • Bottom-dweller preferring sand, silt, or mud
  • Peak reproduction in July and early-August
  • Can live up to 7 years old

Diet

  • Worms
  • Amphipods and other small crustaceans
  • Small fish

Role in the Hudson

  • Important predators in benthic food web

Why we monitor

  • Close connection to the bottom can indicate water quality and habitat health

Rainbow Smelt

Osmerus mordax

Did you know?

When this fish is taken out of the water, it gives off the odor of freshly cut cucumbers

Appearance

  • Elongate and slender
  • Pointed head
  • Large mouth
  • Deeply forked caudal fin
  • Bright silvery stripe along body

Life history

  • Reproduce in the spring in freshwater at night
  • Can produce an antifreeze protein to enable survival at subfreezing temperatures

Diet

  • Aquatic insects, worms, small crustaceans, fish

Role in the Hudson

  • Prey for larger predatory fish and birds

Why we monitor

  • Broader ecosystem level changes can be revealed by tracking population changes

Shortnose Sturgeon

Acipenser brevirostrum

Did you know?

The oldest female ever recorded was 67 years old and the oldest male was 32 years old

Appearance

  • Short conical snout with 4 barbels in front of the mouth
  • Five rows of scutes: one on the back, two on the belly, and one on each side

Life history

  • Reproduction is associated with gravel-dominated bottom-types
  • Reproduction in the Hudson River occurs from April to May

Diet

  • Worms, insect larvae, plants, snails, shrimp, crayfish

Role in the Hudson

  • Benthic scavenger regulating invertebrate populations

Why we monitor

  • Listed under the Endangered Species Act
  • Maintain recovery of the population in the Hudson River

Spottail Shiner

Notropis hudsonius hudsonius

Did you know?

The Spottail Shiner was named after the Hudson River!

Their scientific name, hudsonius, means “of the Hudson.”

Appearance

  • Small, slender, silvery fish
  • Distinct black spot at base of tail
  • Average size ranges 2-3 inches

Life history

  • Small, schooling fish found in freshwater and brackish habitats
  • Spawn during warmer months (June-July)

Diet

  • Omnivores. Eat small aquatic organisms including insect larvae, crustaceans, and zooplankton

Role in the Hudson

  • Forage fish in the Hudson River food web
  • Key food source for larger predatory fish and wading birds

Why we monitor

  • Important forage fish species
  • Tracking changes in the population over time helps reveal broader changes in the Hudson River ecosystem

Tessellated Darter

Etheostoma olmstedi

Did you know?

Darters get their name from the way they quickly “dart” along the river bottom!

Appearance

  • Small fish; slender body
  • Brownish-tan with dark, W-shaped markings
  • Two fins along back
  • Grows 2-4 inches

Life history

  • Bottom dwelling; found in shallow, slow-moving waters
  • Uses large pectoral fins to help rest and move on bottom
  • Spawn in spring

Diet

  • Small crustaceans
  • Insects and insect larvae
  • Snails
  • Algae

Role in the Hudson

  • Serve as food source for larger fish and birds
  • Help to regulate invertebrate populations

Why we monitor

  • Their close connection to bottom habitats can help indicate local water quality and habitat health

White Catfish

Ameiurus catus

Did you know?

White Catfish can live for more than a decade!

Appearance

  • Smooth, scaleless body
  • Broad, flattened head
  • Bluish-gray back with pale belly
  • Four pairs of whisker-like barbels
  • Forked tail

Life history

  • Bottom dwelling freshwater fish
  • Most active evening through nighttime
  • Builds nests; guards young

Diet

  • Opportunistic feeder
  • Aquatic insects, crustaceans, small fish, mollusks

Role in the Hudson

  • Adult fish are important predators
  • Young white catfish are an important prey species for larger predatory fish such as the Striped Bass

Why we monitor

  • As bottom dwellers, they reflect local water and habitat quality.
  • Can accumulate pollutants like heavy metals, helping inform safe fish consumption advisories

White Perch

Morone americana

Did you know?

In the Hudson River, there are both resident and migratory populations

Appearance

  • Deep, laterally compressed body. Can grow up to 22 inches. Silvery-white, with two dorsal fins slightly joined by a membrane.

Life history

  • Semi-anadromous
  • R-selected species
  • Reaches maturity at ~ 2-4 years

Diet

  • Ranges from fish eggs, invertebrates, to small fish like minnows

Role in the Hudson

  • Prolific mid-trophic level species linking lower and higher trophic level species

Why we monitor

  • Helps inform ecosystem–based management

3 Plan your visits and gear

One station-visit means one visit to one station. Gear costs repeat at each station-visit.

4 Choose measurements

Core catch or detection data come from your method. Add measurements that help answer your question.

5 Defend your design

Discussion after your presentations
  1. Which groups prioritized spatial coverage? Which prioritized repeated sampling?
  2. How did the research question change the “best” gear choice?
  3. Where did budget constraints force the biggest scientific compromise?
  4. Which designs could estimate relative abundance? Which mainly detect presence?
  5. What would happen if everyone used a different method every year?
  6. What information would managers still need after these surveys were completed?

Fish Species

Alewife

Alosa pseudoharengus

A schooling river herring that migrates into fresh water to spawn in spring. It feeds on zooplankton and small aquatic animals and provides prey for larger fish and birds.

Chesapeake Bay Program

American Shad

Alosa sapidissima

A migratory herring that spends much of its life at sea and enters freshwater rivers to spawn. Juveniles grow in river nurseries before moving toward coastal waters.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Atlantic Cutlassfish

Trichiurus lepturus

A marine predator with a long, ribbon-like body and a tapering tail. Also called ribbonfish, it feeds mainly on smaller fish and shrimp in coastal waters.

Texas Parks and Wildlife

Atlantic Menhaden

Brevoortia tyrannus

A schooling coastal herring that filters plankton from the water. Its young use estuaries as nursery habitat, and it is important prey for many larger fishes and seabirds.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Atlantic Silverside

Menidia menidia

A small schooling fish recognized by the bright silver stripe along its side. It occupies shallow coastal waters and feeds on zooplankton and other small invertebrates.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Atlantic Tomcod

Microgadus tomcod

A small bottom-dwelling member of the cod family found in cool coastal waters, estuaries, and tidal rivers. It moves into upper tidal reaches to spawn in autumn and winter.

Connecticut DEEP

Bay Anchovy

Anchoa mitchilli

A small schooling fish with a translucent body and a large mouth. It feeds primarily on zooplankton and links plankton production to larger estuarine predators.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Blueback Herring

Alosa aestivalis

A river herring that moves between coastal waters and freshwater spawning grounds. Schools feed on plankton and small crustaceans and support many predatory fish and birds.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Bluefish

Pomatomus saltatrix

An active coastal predator with strong jaws and sharp teeth. It feeds on schooling fishes and squid, and its young use estuarine waters during the warmer months.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Butterfish

Peprilus triacanthus

A small, deep-bodied marine fish that forms loose schools and feeds on small invertebrates. It grows quickly and shifts between inshore and offshore waters with seasonal temperature changes.

NOAA Fisheries

Channel Catfish

Ictalurus punctatus

A bottom-feeding catfish with a deeply forked tail and sensory barbels around its mouth. It inhabits rivers and brackish waters and eats a wide variety of aquatic animals.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Freshwater Drum

Aplodinotus grunniens

A bottom-dwelling fish of rivers and lakes, noted for its drumming or grunting sounds. Strong throat teeth help it consume mollusks; it also eats insects, crustaceans, and fish.

Texas Parks and Wildlife

Inshore Lizardfish

Synodus foetens

A coastal bottom predator with a long body, broad mouth, and sharp teeth. It can bury itself in sand or mud and ambush small fishes and invertebrates.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Spottail Shiner

Hudsonius hudsonius

A small schooling minnow with a dark spot at the base of its tail. It inhabits rivers and lake shores, often over sand, and eats insects, crustaceans, and plant material.

FishBase · Connecticut DEEP

Spotted Hake

Urophycis regia

A bottom-dwelling hake found along the northwestern Atlantic coast. Juveniles spend time in estuaries, and the species feeds mainly on crustaceans, with fish and squid also reported.

FishBase

Striped Bass

Morone saxatilis

A coastal predator identified by dark horizontal stripes along its sides. It moves upstream to spawn in fresh or brackish water, and young fish develop in estuarine nursery areas.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Tessellated Darter

Etheostoma olmstedi

A small bottom-dwelling fish found in streams, rivers, and lake margins. It makes short darting movements while feeding on tiny aquatic animals; males guard eggs laid beneath stones.

Chesapeake Bay Program

Weakfish

Cynoscion regalis

A coastal predator that feeds on small fishes, shrimp, and other invertebrates. Young weakfish use low-salinity estuarine nursery areas before moving into saltier waters as they grow.

Chesapeake Bay Program

White Catfish

Ameiurus catus

A catfish native to Atlantic coastal drainages that inhabits slow-flowing rivers, lakes, and brackish waters. It has a broad head and moderately forked tail and eats fishes, invertebrates, and plant material.

Chesapeake Bay Program

White Perch

Morone americana

A small, silvery fish that lives in fresh and brackish waters. It moves upstream to spawn in spring and feeds on small fishes, aquatic insects, and fish eggs.

Chesapeake Bay Program
